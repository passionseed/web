## 4 things teenagers told us about our camp

_founder · 2026-10-11 · attach li-students-told-us/_

We asked high schoolers what was wrong with our camp. They did not hold back.

SHIFT is a 7-day online sprint. Students pick a problem, build a prototype with AI tools, and put it in front of real people outside the program. After each round we ask what didn't work.

Four things we heard, and what we changed:

1. "Getting outsiders to try it takes time." So every student now writes down who they will ask on Day 1, and we teach direct one-person asks instead of posting to a story and hoping.

2. "It's too easy, you just tell the AI, so I got lazy." So we added a rule we call Anti Meat Proxy: use AI as much as you want, but you question it, you decide, and you own the result.

3. "Saying you can build a tool in one day makes people think it's a scam." So we stopped promising apps. We promise a prototype that strangers can try. Nothing bigger.

4. "I'm an introvert, I never make friends at camps." So students can build solo or in a team of up to three, and every evening the whole room reviews work together.

The pattern: teenagers are sharp reviewers when you actually let them review you. All four quotes are in the carousel in their original Thai.

## Give students the fishing rod, not the fish

_founder · 2026-10-11 · attach li-fishing-rod/_

Our students build with AI coding tools. We still don't let the AI do the work.

When a 16-year-old can generate an app in an afternoon, the risk is not that they can't build. The risk is that they never decide anything. They become a relay between the AI and the screen.

So the coach we built for SHIFT students, SeedStack, runs on three rules:

• It asks before it builds. Who is the user? What exactly is the problem? What are you cutting?
• The student runs every command. The AI points to the official install page. The student types it.
• Decisions stay with the student: scope, who to test with, and what to change after it breaks.

AI made the building part fast. Talking to real users, cutting scope, and owning what broke is still slow, human work. That is the part worth teaching.

How are you handling AI tools with students? I'd like to compare notes.

## A student told us our ad read like a scam

_founder · 2026-10-11_

A high school student read our poster and told us, politely, that it sounded like a scam.

“เขียนว่าทำเครื่องมือได้ในวันเดียว คนจะคิดว่าหลอก”
(translated) "If you say you can build a tool in one day, people will think it's a scam."

The student was right. We were selling the most exciting version of the week instead of the true one.

So we rewrote everything. We promise a prototype that people outside the program can try. Not an app, not a startup, not an admission result. Every claim and number on a poster now has a source we can point to, or it comes off.

Teenagers have very good detectors for hype, and their parents have better ones. The honest version is the one they can check.

If you market to students: what's the claim on your page you couldn't prove tomorrow?

## Building is no longer the bottleneck. Distribution is.

_founder · 2026-10-11_

In our earliest rounds, building was not where students got stuck. They got stuck on something we didn't plan for: nobody tried what they built.

They posted to their stories, some to close friends only, and waited. Almost nothing came back. Asking everyone at once means nobody feels asked.

With AI tools, building a prototype is no longer the hard part for a 16-year-old. Getting ten strangers to use it is.

So we now teach distribution like any other skill:
• Day 1: write the names of the people you will ask, not a post you will publish
• Ask one person at a time, say why it's them, ask for five minutes
• Write down their words, not your summary

It's uncomfortable, and it's the most transferable thing they learn all week.

## The best student project idea was sitting in the living room

_founder · 2026-10-11_

One of our students watched their grandpa struggle to scan a QR code to pay.

“เห็นคุณตาที่บ้านสแกน QR Code ไม่เป็น ถ้าไม่เปิด LINE สแกนไม่ถูก ผมเลยทำแอปช่วยคนแก่ สแกนเด้งเข้าแอปธนาคารได้เลย”
(translated) "My grandpa couldn't scan QR codes unless he opened LINE first, so I made an app for older people: scan, and it jumps straight into the bank app."

No brainstorming workshop produced that idea. The student saw the problem first-hand, and the first tester lived in the same house.

When students say they "have no ideas", we no longer ask them to think bigger. We ask: who at home asks you for help with the same thing every week?

## They come for the portfolio. They leave having taught themselves.

_founder · 2026-10-11_

Thai students don't sign up for SHIFT to "find themselves". They sign up because they need something real for their university portfolio. We think that's fine.

The portfolio gets them in the door. What happens inside is different. A student who calls themself an introvert went and interviewed strangers, because the project needed it. Afterwards they told us:

“งานที่โรงเรียนครูคอยทำให้ … แต่ค่ายนี้เราต้องค้นเองหมด ทำให้รู้ว่าไม่ต้องมีครูก็ได้ เราหาความรู้เองได้”
(translated) "At school the teacher does it for us … but here we had to find everything ourselves. It showed me I don't need a teacher. I can learn on my own."

That sentence is the whole point. Autonomy, competence, and a room of peers who treat building as normal. Self-Determination Theory, delivered inside a portfolio project.

Sell the outcome people already want. Deliver the one they didn't know to ask for.

## A certificate proves attendance

_founder · 2026-10-11_

A certificate proves you attended. A prototype someone used proves you can do something.

Thai high schoolers collect certificates for their TCAS portfolios because that's what the market sells them. Many of those camps are lectures: everyone sits, everyone gets the same paper.

What can't be copied is different:
• the exact words of the people you interviewed
• a link that opens
• a dated log of what broke and what you changed
• one number, before and after

One project in our pilot round started as a portfolio-criteria website. After talking to the people who would use it, the student learned they cared far more about exam dates, changed the brief, and built a countdown calendar instead.

That story is worth more in an interview than ten certificates, because nobody else has it.
